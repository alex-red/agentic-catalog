# Creating Novel Designs

## Find the material

Identify who the design serves, what they should understand or do, and which brand, format, and usability constraints apply. Look through the brief and available assets for things specific to this project. A real object, a repeated action, a telling piece of data, or a customer's own words can carry more of the design than a style adjective.

If the brief is thin and the answer would change the direction, ask what people actually handle, see, or do in that setting, or what the user is tired of seeing in the category. With enough material or a request to proceed, work from what is available and identify consequential assumptions.

## Turn it into a design decision

Choose one detail that expresses something the audience needs to understand. Extract a property you can reuse, such as a sequence, a physical relationship, a marking system, or a way of comparing information. If the obvious translation feels like a costume, look to a different field that solves a similar communication problem. Borrow one principle, such as how a timetable groups departures, and apply it to the project's own content.

State the concept in one sentence that connects the source detail, a visible design choice, and what it helps the audience understand or do. Put the idea into the composition and a supporting choice where it earns its place. Concentrate the strongest expression where it helps the most. Use familiar controls and reading order for repeated or consequential tasks.

For a tool-lending library, take the prominent date from a checkout slip and give it a new job. Lead with verified availability on an item's page and align the dates in search results so people can scan for tools they can borrow this weekend. Use familiar checkout controls to confirm the pickup and return dates.

When direction is open and the decision matters, sketch a few genuinely different approaches, from familiar to bolder. Try organizing around the content itself, a real sequence or relationship, or an action that reveals how the product works. Compare the same key surface with the same content and level of finish. Recommend the direction that best serves the audience with the assets available. Describe what people would see first, why that helps, and what the direction costs in clarity, assets, or effort. Develop the supplied direction directly when it already settles the choice. To make an existing direction bolder, first try giving its main idea more room or emphasis within the visual system.

## Check the result in its medium

Inspect the applied work at its intended size. If the concept has to work across surfaces or states, try it with real content where space, density, or interaction makes it hardest to carry through. Check what reads first and whether the main action or message stays clear. Try swapping in another organization's name. If the defining elements would still fit, replace interchangeable choices with subject-specific ones that serve the same purpose. Temporarily remove the strongest flourish and check what you lose. Keep it if it carries meaning, helps the task, or contributes to the intended tone. Remove details without a job.

Use supplied facts and assets where possible. Label sample data and stand-ins. Report any material assumptions or checks you could not perform.
